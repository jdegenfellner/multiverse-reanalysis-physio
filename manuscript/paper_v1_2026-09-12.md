# Reproducibility without a mandate: how far can a reader recompute exercise, rehabilitation and physiotherapy trials from their public data, and how far do their conclusions move?

## Abstract

**Background.** Computational reproduction at scale has been shown where journals mandate
data and code. Physiotherapy, rehabilitation and exercise science have no mandate. We
asked what a reader can recompute from public deposits unaided, whether published
estimates reproduce, and how far they move over analytical choices classified in advance
as equivalent or as changing the estimand.

**Methods.** From 423 open-access records in Europe PMC we identified 152 studies with a
repository link in their availability statement, retrieved the deposits, and assessed
the 48 randomised trials with a readable data file under fixed rules for identifying the
primary outcome, control arm and published model. We recomputed the published primary
estimate and ran two prespecified multiverses: a principled grid of choices with the
same estimand, and a defensible grid adding missing-data strategy, logarithmic
transformation and arm-wise outlier rules. Effects are in pooled within-group standard
deviations.

**Results.** Sixteen trials were included, 11% of those with a link. Of 32 exclusions,
11 were trials whose public, readable data could not support the published analysis.
Ten of 12 published point estimates were recovered to 2% or 0.02 standard deviations.
Over 395 principled specifications the range of the
standardised effect had a median of 0.15 standard deviations (bootstrap interval 0.08
to 0.23) and a maximum of 1.08; the sign was stable in 15 of 16 trials. Baseline handling
had the largest share of the estimate variance in 10 of 15 trials; with it fixed, the
median range was 0.003. The defensible grid widened the range by a median factor of
1.35, mostly through logarithmic transformation. Nine of 16 papers named no primary
outcome, four reported no point estimate, one stated an estimand. Our first mapping of
analyses onto data reversed the effect direction in 7 of 16 trials before rules were fixed.

**Conclusions.** Without a mandate, one trial in nine with a repository link can be
recomputed by a reader, and the loss occurs after the data are public. Where
recomputation is possible it works. Fragility over equivalent choices is small and almost
entirely the choice of baseline handling; most of what a wider multiverse adds is a
change of question.

## 1. Introduction

Computational reproduction of published research at scale has been demonstrated where
journals require authors to deposit data and code. Brodeur and colleagues reproduced 110
articles from economics and political science journals with mandatory data and code
policies and found more than 85% of them computationally reproducible; 72% of
statistically significant estimates remained significant with the same sign under
robustness checks chosen by the reproducers [Brodeur_2026]. A policy that is not verified
at submission does less: under the data and code policy of Science, artefacts could be
obtained for 44% of a sample of articles and results reproduced for 26% [Stodden_2018].
Biomedical audits have stalled one step earlier.
Naudet and colleagues identified 37 randomised trials published under the data-sharing
policies of the BMJ and PLOS Medicine, obtained data for 17, reproduced the primary
result fully in 14 and reached the same conclusion in 16 [Naudet_2018]. For trials
assessed by the European Medicines Agency, Siebert and colleagues could reanalyse 10 of 62
(16%) [Siebert_2022]. In psychology, about a third of the datasets shared under the
mandatory policy of Cognition were not reusable [Hardwicke_2018]; among articles with
reusable data and an open data badge at Psychological Science, the obstacle to
reproduction was unclear reporting of the analysis [Hardwicke_2021].

Physiotherapy and rehabilitation have no data-sharing mandate. In the journals of four
national physiotherapy associations, 12% of 465 articles made data directly available
[Jabouille_2025]. When authors of rehabilitation studies whose availability statements
promised data on request were asked, 22.7% complied [Elghzali_2025]. In sports and
exercise science, 14% of authors provided raw data on request, and 28% of attempted
replications succeeded [Murphy_2025]. Dhanani and colleagues recomputed the statistics of
572 trials from the numbers printed in the articles, without individual data
[Dhanani_2022]. We are not aware of a study in physiotherapy, rehabilitation or exercise
science that retrieved individual participant data from public repositories and
recomputed the published primary analysis. Reproducibility in this literature usually
means whether a clinician can reproduce an exercise programme from its description; we
mean computational reproducibility, the recovery of the published number from the
published data and methods.

A recomputed estimate answers one question: was the reported number obtained by the
reported method. A second question is how far the number depends on choices that the
method leaves open. Multiverse analysis and specification curve analysis address this
question by computing every defensible combination of analytical choices [Steegen_2016;
Simonsohn_2020]. The method is popular and loosely applied. Of 613 multiverse-style
studies, the median study computed 144 specifications; among the 152 coded in depth,
8.6% were preregistered, 3.9% discussed whether their specifications were equivalent or
defensible, and 82.2% reported the results descriptively without any inferential summary
[Nepomuceno_2026].
Del Giudice and Gangestad showed that a multiverse padded with options that are not
equivalent can obscure a real effect, and proposed classifying each decision as Type E,
where the options have comparable validity, examine the same effect and estimate it with
comparable precision; Type N, where one
option has justified precedence or the options answer different questions; or Type U,
where equivalence is uncertain [Del_Giudice_2021]. Short and colleagues separate the
defensibility of a single pipeline from the equivalence of pipelines with one another,
and warn that a multiverse whose pipelines produce nearly identical datasets creates an
illusion of consistency [Short_2026]. In a simulated behavioural trial, Veltri found that
preprocessing choices explained 76.9% of the variance of the effect estimate and model
choices 7.5%, but the simulation did not separate choices that change the estimand from
choices that do not [Veltri_2025]. Crenshaw and colleagues applied the multiverse
approach to one clinical trial [Crenshaw_2026]. The epidemiological version of the same
question, vibration of effects, has been studied for observational associations
[Patel_2015; Klau_2020], and studies in which many analysts receive the same data have
shown how far conclusions vary between competent analysts [Silberzahn_2018;
Botvinik_Nezer_2020; Breznau_2022; Gould_2025]. None of these applied one prespecified,
equivalence-classified grid to a corpus of trials.

We asked four questions. First, of trials in exercise, rehabilitation and physiotherapy
with publicly deposited data, in how many can the published primary analysis be
identified in the data and recomputed by a reader who contacts no one, and where is the
loss? Second, does the published primary estimate reproduce? Third, over a prespecified
grid of choices classified as equivalent, how far does the standardised effect move, and
which choice moves it? Fourth, how much range does a wider grid of defensible but
non-equivalent choices add, and through which choices? We also record which reporting
features of the papers obstructed reproduction, and we report how often our own first
mapping of published analyses onto deposited data was wrong before we fixed rules for it.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Design and transparency

We conducted a meta-research audit of randomised trials in exercise, rehabilitation and
physiotherapy that had deposited individual participant data in a public repository.
The audit has three stages. Stage 1 asks in how many trials the published primary
analysis can be identified in the deposited data and recomputed without contacting
anyone. Stage 2 asks whether the published primary estimate reproduces. Stage 3 applies
the same prespecified grid of analytical choices to every trial and measures how far the
estimate moves.

The audit was not preregistered. The grid of decision nodes was written on 28 August
2026, before any specification was computed. The rules that map a published analysis
onto the deposited data (section 2.3) were fixed after we had extracted the published
analyses from all candidate papers and before the final computation. All results were
inspected before this manuscript was written. Brodeur et al. state that their own
reanalyses "should thus all be considered as not pre-registered" because the
implementable checks cannot be known before a replication package is opened
[Brodeur_2026, check wording]; the same applies here. Section 3.5 reports what the
rule-based mapping changed relative to our first, ad hoc mapping. The transparency
items proposed by van den Akker et al. for secondary data analysis are addressed in
Supplement Text S1 [Van_den_Akker_2021]. Code, the trial registry, the grid definition and
all outputs are available at [repository DOI to be created].

### 2.2 Corpus

We searched Europe PMC on [date, check README] for open-access articles with
physiotherapy, rehabilitation or exercise-therapy terms in the title or abstract, a
clinical design (publication type randomised controlled trial, clinical trial or
observational study, or corresponding title terms), and a repository term anywhere in
the full text. The search returned 423 records. For each record we retrieved the JATS
full text and extracted the data- and code-availability statement. A record was
retained when a repository URL appeared inside that statement. Counting URLs anywhere in
the full text had inflated an earlier pass with citations of software repositories;
restricting the count to the availability statement reduced apparent matches with both
code and data from 107 [check: number from the first screening pass, README] to 4. The restricted count gave 152 studies, 91 of them
randomised trials.

For every retained study we queried the application programming interfaces of OSF,
Zenodo, Dryad and figshare, listed the deposit and classified files by extension. A
deposit counted as reachable when a file list was returned, and as containing data when
at least one tabular file (csv, xlsx, sav, rds or similar) was present. Two properties of
these interfaces produce silent empty results and are documented in the code: Zenodo
limits unauthenticated requests to 25 records per page and 30 requests per minute, and
OSF requires percent-encoded pagination parameters and returns folders before files.
The deposit probe left 84 reachable deposits, 74 with a tabular file, 48 of them
randomised trials. These 48 were downloaded and assessed by hand.

A second search route, querying Zenodo and Dryad directly for clinically relevant
deposits, returned 127 study-like deposits. These were not matched to articles for this
report and are discussed as an extension in section 4.7.

### 2.3 Eligibility and mapping rules

A trial entered stages 2 and 3 when four conditions held: it was a randomised trial of an
exercise, rehabilitation or physiotherapy intervention; individual participant data were
downloadable without application or data-use agreement; the primary outcome and the
allocation were identifiable in the data; and the published methods described the
primary analysis in enough detail to reconstruct it. Group-level summary tables did not
count as individual data. We did not contact authors.

Design exclusions were applied to all 48 trials in the same way: crossover and
within-person designs, because the grid assumes parallel groups; feasibility studies
whose primary aim was recruitment or retention; studies without a clinical or training
intervention; non-randomised studies; a binary primary outcome, because the grid is built
for continuous outcomes; and secondary analyses of an included trial that report no arm
contrast. Every exclusion is recorded with its category and reason in Supplement Table
S1.

Mapping a published analysis onto deposited data requires decisions about which variable
is the primary outcome, which timepoint is primary, which value of the group variable is
the control arm, and which covariates entered the published model. Our first mapping
made these decisions study by study. After the published analyses had been extracted
from all 21 candidate papers with verbatim quotations, we fixed eight rules and rebuilt
the mapping under them. The primary outcome is the one named in the trial registration;
failing that, the one the paper calls primary; failing that, the first outcome reported in
the abstract; failing that, the outcome of the sample-size calculation. The source is
recorded for each trial. The primary timepoint is the one the paper designates, otherwise
the first follow-up after the end of the intervention. When the primary outcome or its
timepoint is absent from the deposit, the trial is excluded; no substitute outcome is
used. The control arm is the arm the paper describes as control, placebo, sham,
wait-list or usual care; its code in the data is verified through value labels or, when
labels are absent, by matching group sizes and baseline means against the paper's first
table. Covariates are those that appear as adjustment terms in the published primary
model; variables tested only for baseline balance do not count. Each paper is classified
as a primary report, which reports the trial's prespecified primary analysis, or a
secondary report, which presents an exploratory or secondary analysis with its own
headline estimate. Field and population are coded for each trial. A second coder is to
repeat the mapping blind to the first, and agreement per field will be reported [check:
pending].

### 2.4 Stage 2: computational reproduction

From each included paper we extracted the published primary estimate, its interval and
p-value, and the specification of the published analysis (baseline handling, covariate
set, analysis population, missing-data strategy, model family, standard-error method,
transformation), each with a verbatim quotation. Where a paper reported only a test
statistic or a p-value, the trial was classed as having no point estimate. Two documented
adjustments were made to the extracted values. For the time-restricted eating trial the
paper reports both a time-by-group interaction and a direct post-intervention contrast
between the two arms; we used the direct contrast because it corresponds to the estimand
of our grid. For the phosphatidic acid trial the value first extracted was an F statistic,
and the trial was reclassed as having no point estimate.

Each published specification was matched to the specification in the defensible grid
(section 2.6) that agreed with it on the largest number of nodes. Published model
families absent from the grid, such as random-slope models, Bayesian multilevel models
and non-parametric tests, counted as non-matches. Ties were broken by the specification
with the estimate closest to the published one; this rule can only favour agreement, and
we report whether a tie occurred. Whether all scored nodes matched is reported
separately as "specification representable in the grid".

Agreement between the published and the recomputed estimate was classified on two
criteria, because a relative criterion alone is unstable when the published estimate is
close to zero. A reproduction was classed as full when the recomputed estimate had the
same sign and differed by at most 2% or by at most 0.02 standard deviations of the raw
outcome; as a minor discrepancy when it differed by at most 10% or 0.05 standard
deviations; and as a major discrepancy otherwise. Hardwicke et al. used a relative
criterion with minor discrepancies below 10% and major discrepancies at or above 10%
[Hardwicke_2021]; Naudet et al. considered and rejected a 2% threshold [Naudet_2018].
Both criteria are reported for every trial, and the classification under the relative
criterion alone is given as a sensitivity analysis. We did not judge whether the
published analysis was appropriate.

### 2.5 Data integrity screen

Before any multiverse was computed, each dataset was screened for patterns that would
make a fragility analysis meaningless. For every continuous baseline variable named in
the paper we computed a one-way analysis of variance p-value for the difference between
arms. Under correct randomisation these p-values are uniform [Carlisle_2017]; they were
combined by Stouffer's method, which suits a two-sided question better than Fisher's
combination because arms that are too similar and arms that are too different are both
of interest. A combined value below 0.005 or above 0.995 was treated as a triage flag,
not as a verdict. The test was applied to continuous variables only; Bolland et al.
showed that rounding distorts the distribution of these p-values, and the deposited
baseline variables are rounded to varying degrees, which we did not correct
[Bolland_2019]. Duplicate rows over all numeric columns were counted. A terminal-digit
test on the outcome was computed but not used for flagging, because several primary
outcomes are derived means whose terminal digits are structurally non-uniform. Flagged
trials would have been excluded from stage 3 and reported separately. At 16 trials with
few named baseline variables the screen has low power, and a negative result carries
little information.

### 2.6 Stage 3: two grids

The multiverse varies eight decision nodes (Table 1). Following Del Giudice and
Gangestad, each node was classified before computation as Type E, where the options have
comparable validity, examine the same effect and estimate it with comparable precision;
Type N, where one option has justified precedence or the options examine different
effects; or Type U, where equivalence is uncertain [Del_Giudice_2021]. Type E nodes form the principled grid. Type E and Type N nodes
together form the defensible grid, in the sense of Short et al.: every option is used in
the literature and can be justified on its own, without the requirement of equivalence
[Short_2026]. The difference between the two grids is a result of the audit, not a
robustness check.

The Type E nodes are the handling of the baseline value (follow-up value, change score,
or analysis of covariance with the baseline as covariate), all three unbiased for the same
mean difference under randomisation [Vickers_2001; Senn_2006; Van_Breukelen_2006]; the
covariate set (none, the stratification factors, or
the published set) [Kahan_2014; Kahan_2012]; winsorising of outliers pooled over arms
(none, 3 standard deviations, 1.5 interquartile ranges, 3 median absolute deviations),
applied to outcome and baseline alike; the model family (linear model, or a random
intercept for a cluster variable where one is estimable); and the inference method
(model-based standard error, heteroscedasticity-consistent HC3 standard error, or a
permutation test with 4 999 permutations). The Type N nodes are the missing-data strategy
(complete cases; multiple imputation by predictive mean matching with 20 imputations,
Rubin's rules and Barnard and Rubin degrees of freedom; or last observation carried
forward where a baseline exists), classed as Type N because multiple imputation has
justified precedence under a missing-at-random assumption and last observation carried
forward assumes no change after dropout [White_2011]; logarithmic transformation of
outcome and baseline, which estimates a ratio of geometric means; and arm-wise instead of
pooled winsorising, which uses post-randomisation information. No Type U node was
retained in the computed grids.

One classification departs from the strict criteria. The three ways of handling the
baseline value estimate the same quantity but not with the same precision: analysis of
covariance is more efficient than the change score, which is more efficient than the
follow-up value when baseline and outcome are correlated [Vickers_2001; Senn_2006]. Del
Giudice and Gangestad count a difference in precision as a form of nonequivalence. We
kept baseline handling in the principled grid because all three analyses are used as
primary analyses in this literature and none changes the question, and we report as a
sensitivity analysis the range that remains when baseline handling is fixed to analysis
of covariance.

An option was dropped for a trial when it could not change the data: outlier rules that
winsorised no value, missing-data options without missing outcomes, cluster models
without an estimable cluster variable, logarithms of non-positive values, last
observation carried forward without a baseline. Combinations that are incoherent were
removed: HC3 and permutation inference are defined only for the linear model, and
permutation is not combined with multiple imputation. Specifications that returned
identical estimate, standard error and p-value were collapsed to one, and the share of
collapsed duplicates is reported. Non-applicable nodes are counted per trial. The grid
has 22 options over eight nodes; the number of unique specifications per trial ranges
from 3 to 140 in the principled grid and from 6 to 672 in the defensible grid.

### 2.7 Summary measures

Estimates from different specifications are comparable only on a common scale. Every
estimate was divided by the pooled within-group standard deviation of the raw outcome at
the primary timepoint, computed once per trial before any preprocessing, which gives a
standardised difference d in the convention of Cohen's d and Hedges' g. The residual
standard deviation of the fitted model was not used, because it changes with the
specification and would manufacture range. Specifications on the logarithmic scale were
standardised with the pooled standard deviation of the logarithmic outcome and were never
pooled with raw-scale specifications.

For each trial and grid we report the median, interquartile range and range of d over
unique specifications; the share of specifications whose sign agrees with the median;
the share with |d| above 0.2, Cohen's convention for a small effect, since instrument-
specific minimal important differences were not available for every outcome; the
s-value, the negative base-2 logarithm of the p-value, as a continuous measure of
evidence against the test hypothesis [Rafi_2020]; and the percentile of the published estimate in the
distribution of d. The permutation p-value cannot fall below 1 in 5 000, which caps the
s-value of permutation specifications at 12.3 bits. We do not report the share of
"statistically significant" specifications as a primary measure [Wasserstein_2019].

To identify which node moves the estimate, we fitted for each trial and grid an additive
linear model of d on all varying nodes and partitioned the explained variance by Type II
sums of squares, which do not depend on the order of terms in an unbalanced grid. The
same partition was computed for the standardised width of the 95% interval, because
inference options change the interval but not the estimate. Removing incoherent
combinations confounds some node pairs, in particular model family with inference method;
as a sensitivity analysis we refitted the model with all two-way interactions in the ten
trial-by-grid combinations with at least 30 specifications and checked whether the node
with the largest share changed. The number of trials in which only one node can move the
estimate is reported, because a share of 100% in such a trial is not a finding.

Empirical similarity of the preprocessed datasets was measured by the Pearson correlation
between the outcome vectors of every pair of preprocessing pipelines of a trial. Where
all pairs exceed 0.99, the pipelines barely change the data, and stability of the sign
is not evidence of robustness [Short_2026]. Descriptive correlates of the principled
range (sample size, standardised baseline imbalance, baseline-outcome correlation,
share of missing outcomes) are given as Spearman coefficients over 16 trials without
tests.

Proportions are reported with Wilson 95% intervals. Medians over trials are reported with
95% intervals from 5 000 bootstrap resamples of trials, not of specifications. The
difference between the published estimate and the median of the principled multiverse is
reported as a distribution over trials. Confidence intervals of the published estimate
and of any multiverse summary were not compared for overlap. Pooled estimation over
specifications [Bartos_2025_arXiv] and multiplicity control over specifications
[Mandl_2024] were not performed.

Analyses used R 4.6.0 with the packages dplyr, readxl, haven, lme4, lmerTest, sandwich,
lmtest, mice and car.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Feasibility

Figure 1 shows the corpus. Of 423 records, 152 had a repository link inside the
availability statement (91 randomised trials). Two losses followed. The first is the
availability loss: 68 deposits could not be retrieved through the repository interfaces,
mostly because of truncated or dead links; 10 reachable deposits contained no tabular
file; and 26 of the remaining 74 studies were not randomised. This left 48 randomised
trials with a readable data file, 32% of those with a link. The second loss occurred
after the data had been opened. Of the 48 trials, 17 were excluded for design (crossover
5, not a clinical or training intervention 4, feasibility as the primary aim 3, not
randomised on inspection 2, within-person allocation of limbs 2, binary primary outcome
1), 11 because the deposited data could not support the published primary analysis
(allocation not identifiable 3, primary outcome not identifiable 2, outcome present only
as raw electrophysiological or inertial signals 2, primary timepoint absent 1, summary
table instead of individual data 1, README without data 1, file not machine-readable 1),
2 because the paper reported no arm contrast (a network analysis and a mediation
analysis), and 2 as secondary analyses of a dataset already included. In 11 trials the
data were public and readable and still could not answer the published question.

Sixteen trials entered stages 2 and 3, 33% of the 48 assessed (Wilson 95% interval 22 to
47%) and 11% of the 152 with a link (7 to 16%). Thirteen are primary reports and three
are secondary reports with their own headline estimate. Deposits were hosted on OSF (7),
Zenodo (6) and Dryad (3); two contained analysis code alongside the data. Six trials are
physiotherapy or rehabilitation trials in patients or survivors, one a neuromodulation
trial in patients, one a corticosteroid injection trial for frozen shoulder, six
training or nutrition trials in healthy adults, and two exercise trials in pregnancy.
Eight interventions were delivered by physiotherapists. The trials randomised 18 to 404
participants; the number analysable for the primary contrast ranged from 16 to 294.

### 3.2 Reproduction

Twelve of the 16 papers report a point estimate for the primary arm contrast (Table 3).
Ten were recovered to 2% or 0.02 standard deviations (83%, Wilson interval 55 to
95%), two with a minor discrepancy, and none with a major discrepancy. The two minor
discrepancies are the frozen shoulder trial (6.9%, 0.063 standard deviations; the paper
pools two follow-ups in one repeated-measures model, we analysed the later one) and the
FREE trial of low back pain (29%, 0.040 standard deviations; the published model includes
a random slope and covariates that are not all in the deposit). Under the relative
criterion alone, six reproductions are full, three minor and three major; the three
relative "major" discrepancies are absolute differences of 0.016 to 0.040 standard
deviations in trials whose published estimate is close to zero. Absolute differences
range from 0.000 to 0.063 standard deviations over the 12 trials.

The published specification could be represented completely in the grid in 7 of 12
trials (58%, 32 to 81%). The non-representable elements are a random-slope model, a
Bayesian multilevel model, a longitudinal model over 14 measurement occasions with an
autoregressive covariance on the logarithmic scale, a constrained longitudinal analysis
with a centre effect, and a three-arm mixed model.

The published estimate lies inside the range of the principled multiverse in 9 of 12
trials (75%, 47 to 91%) and inside the range of the defensible multiverse in all 12 (76
to 100%). The difference between the published estimate and the median of the principled
multiverse has a median of 0.006 standard deviations over the 12 trials and ranges from
−0.055 to 0.163 (Supplement Figure S18).

Counting every study with a repository link as the denominator, as Siebert et al. did for
all sampled trials [Siebert_2022], the published primary estimate was recovered in 12 of
152 (8%, 5 to 13%); counting the 48 assessed trials, in 12 of 48 (25%, 15 to 39%).

### 3.3 Fragility over equivalent choices

The principled grid yielded 395 unique specifications over 16 trials, between 3 and 140
per trial, after collapsing 4.4% exact duplicates. Figure 2 shows the median and range of
d for every trial, and Figure 3 shows the specification curves of three trials. The range of d over equivalent choices has a median of 0.15 standard
deviations (bootstrap 95% interval 0.08 to 0.23; interquartile range 0.07 to 0.23). The
largest range is 1.08 standard deviations, in the transcranial direct current stimulation
trial with 20 participants; the next largest are 0.41 and 0.39. The sign of d was the same
in every specification in 15 of 16 trials (72 to 99%); in one trial, FREE, the range
includes zero. Per-trial median s-values range from 0.5 to 19.1 bits (median 5.2). Every specification
gave |d| above 0.2 in 8 trials and none did in 4; in 4 trials the share lay between.

Baseline handling had the largest share of the estimate variance in 10 of the 15 trials
in which any node varied, the outlier rule in 3 and the covariate set in 2 (Figure 4).
This dominance is partly structural: in 7 of the 15 trials only baseline handling and the
inference method vary in the principled grid, and the inference method cannot move the
estimate, which leaves baseline handling with a share of 100% by default. For the width
of the 95% interval (Supplement Figure S17), baseline handling had the largest share in 10 trials, the outlier
rule in 3, the covariate set in 1 and the inference method in 1; the inference method
accounted for a median of 3.7% of the interval-width variance. Refitting with all two-way
interactions in the ten trial-by-grid combinations with at least 30 specifications did
not change the node with the largest share in any of them; interaction terms accounted
for a median of 9% of the explained variance (Supplement Table S4). With baseline
handling fixed to analysis of covariance, the range of d over the remaining equivalent
choices has a median of 0.003 standard deviations and exceeds 0.05 in four trials: FREE
0.11, water exercise 0.08, TERECO 0.05, and the transcranial stimulation trial 1.03, where
the outlier rule drives the range (Supplement Table S5).

The principled range was not associated with sample size (Spearman −0.06 over 16 trials)
or with the share of missing outcomes (0.02). It was weakly associated with standardised
baseline imbalance between arms (0.41 over the 12 trials with a baseline measurement)
and not with the baseline-outcome correlation (−0.06). These are descriptive coefficients
over few points. In the eight trials in a clinical rehabilitation context the range had a
median of 0.18 standard deviations and a maximum of 1.08; in the eight trials of exercise
physiology or sports nutrition the median was 0.06 and the maximum 0.41.

In 9 of the 16 trials every pair of preprocessing pipelines produced outcome vectors
correlated above 0.99; in these trials the stability of the sign says little about
robustness, because the options barely change the data. The integrity screen flagged no
trial.

### 3.4 Estimand versus analysis

The defensible grid yielded 1 934 unique specifications, between 6 and 672 per trial,
after collapsing 6.1% exact duplicates. The range of d has a median of 0.28 standard
deviations (bootstrap interval 0.10 to 0.38; interquartile range 0.09 to 0.37) and a
maximum of 1.11, again in the transcranial stimulation trial. The sign was stable in 14
of 16 trials (64 to 97%), and the range includes zero in two, FREE and the ETIP
pregnancy trial. Over the 15 trials with a non-zero principled range, the defensible
range exceeded the principled range by a median factor of 1.35 (bootstrap interval 1.18
to 1.52); in the time-restricted eating trial the principled range is zero because no
Type E node is applicable, and the defensible range is 0.02.

Baseline handling remained the node with the largest share of the estimate variance in 7
of 16 trials. The logarithmic transformation, a Type N node, had the largest share in 5,
the outlier rule in 3 and the covariate set in 1. The missing-data strategy had the
largest share in none.

### 3.5 Reporting, and the effect of our own mapping

Of the 16 papers, one names the primary outcome in a trial registration that the paper
cites, six name it in the methods, and in nine it can only be inferred as the first
outcome reported in the abstract (Table 2). Twelve report a point estimate for the
primary arm contrast; four report only a p-value or an F statistic. One paper states an
estimand. Thirteen report significance tests of baseline balance, and in three the result
of that test determined whether the analysis was adjusted. No paper reports a rule for
outliers. The published baseline handling was analysis of covariance in six, a change
score in four, the follow-up value in two and another approach in four. Model families
were analysis of variance (five), mixed models (six), analysis of covariance (three), a
t-test and a non-parametric test. Seven papers had no missing primary outcomes; of the
remaining nine, four analysed complete cases, three used likelihood-based longitudinal
models, one multiple imputation and one did not say. Two papers modelled clustering (one
by site, one by therapist). A protocol was available for seven trials, a registration
only for six, neither for three. Ten trials appeared in journals that require data
sharing; for six the requirement was unclear.

Our first mapping of published analyses onto deposited data, made study by study before
the rules of section 2.3 were fixed, differed from the rule-based mapping in 13 of the 16
trials: the control arm was coded differently in 10, the outcome variable in 5, the
covariate set in 11, the baseline variable in 3 and the data file in 1 (Supplement Table
S2). Most control-arm differences arose where the first value of the group variable had
been taken as the control arm without checking value labels or group sizes against the
paper. Running the first mapping through the final pipeline gave a median |d| of 0.24
against 0.25 and a median principled range of 0.16 against 0.15, but the sign of the
median d was reversed in 7 of 16 trials. The mapping errors changed the direction of
effects, not their fragility.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

Of 152 trials in exercise, rehabilitation and physiotherapy whose availability
statements pointed to a repository, 16 allowed a reader to identify and recompute the
published primary analysis without contacting anyone; the loss occurred in two steps,
first because deposits could not be reached or read, then because public and readable
data did not contain what the published analysis needed. Where recomputation was
possible, the published estimate was recovered to 2% or 0.02 standard deviations in 10 of
12 trials, and the largest absolute difference was 0.063 standard deviations. Over choices
classified in advance as equivalent, the standardised effect moved by a median of 0.15
standard deviations, by 1.08 in a trial of 20 participants, and kept its sign in 15 of 16
trials; the handling of the baseline value moved it more than any other choice. Adding
choices
that change the estimand widened the range by about a third, mostly through logarithmic
transformation. Nine of the 16 papers name no primary outcome, four give no point
estimate, and one states an estimand; and our own first mapping of published analyses
onto deposited data reversed the direction of the effect in 7 of 16 trials before rules
were fixed.

### 4.2 Two losses, one of them new

The first loss is the one earlier audits describe. Deposits behind truncated links,
archives without a data file and studies that were not trials account for the drop from
152 to 48, and its size is in line with the availability rates of Jabouille and
colleagues and the compliance rates of Elghzali and colleagues [Jabouille_2025;
Elghzali_2025]. The second loss is different in kind. In 11 of 48 trials the data were
public and readable, and the published primary analysis still could not be recovered
from them: the allocation was not in the file, the primary outcome was not
identifiable among the columns, the primary timepoint was missing, or the outcome existed
only as raw signals. These deposits satisfy every availability policy we know of and are
useless for the one purpose that justifies the policy. A deposit that can answer its own
paper's question needs three things that none of these policies require: a variable that
holds the allocation with labelled values, a variable that the paper names as the primary
outcome, and a dictionary that connects the two to the text.

### 4.3 Reproduction

Ten of 12 published estimates were recovered to 2% or 0.02 standard deviations (Wilson
interval 55 to 95%), and the largest absolute difference was 0.063 standard deviations.
The interval is wide because the denominator is 12. The comparison with Brodeur and
colleagues is one of setting, not of rate: they worked under a mandate with 110 articles
and reproducer-chosen checks, we worked without one and reached this step with 12 of 423
screened records. Naudet and colleagues reached the same conclusion as the original
authors in 16 of 17 reanalyses [Naudet_2018]; our discrepancies, where they occurred,
came from model families that the grid cannot represent, such as random slopes or
Bayesian multilevel models, and from covariates that were not in the deposit, not from
inconsistencies in the data. Under a relative criterion alone, three trials would count as
major discrepancies; in each the absolute difference is below 0.05 standard deviations
and the published estimate is close to zero. A relative rule is not informative in that
region, which is why we report both.

### 4.4 Fragility

For the typical trial in this corpus, the choices that a competent analyst could make
without changing the question move the standardised effect by about 0.15 standard
deviations, and the direction does not change. For one trial of 20 participants in which
an outlier rule changes the data, the same choices move it by 1.08. The median describes
the corpus; the maximum describes what a single small trial can do. The node that moves the estimate is the handling of the baseline value, the
choice between follow-up value, change score and analysis of covariance that Vickers and
Altman described a quarter of a century ago [Vickers_2001]. In most trials it is also the only
equivalent choice that can move the estimate at all: covariates, cluster models and
outlier rules were often not applicable, and the inference method changes the interval
and not the estimate. With baseline handling fixed, the median range over the remaining
equivalent choices is 0.003 standard deviations. Whether that residual is what "analytic
fragility" should mean depends on whether one accepts three analyses of unequal
precision as interchangeable; the field does, and a reader of any one of these papers has
to. Baseline imbalance between arms was weakly associated with the
range over 12 trials, in the direction that Senn's and Van Breukelen's analyses predict
[Senn_2006; Van_Breukelen_2006], but 12 points do not support more than that sentence.

The contrast with Veltri's simulation is instructive. There, preprocessing explained
three quarters of the variance of the estimate [Veltri_2025]. In these trials, once
choices that change the estimand are set apart, preprocessing choices contributed little:
outlier rules were applicable in seven trials and dominant in three, missing-data
strategies dominant in none. The one preprocessing choice with a large effect,
logarithmic transformation, estimates a ratio of geometric means and is an estimand
change, not an analytic one. The defensible grid exceeded the principled grid by a median
factor of 1.35, and that factor is our measure of how much apparent fragility in an
unclassified multiverse would have been ambiguity about the question. Crenshaw and
colleagues showed what a multiverse looks like for one trial [Crenshaw_2026]; applying one
classified grid to 16 trials is what makes the pattern visible as a property of the
literature and not of a dataset.

Two cautions apply. In 9 of 16 trials the preprocessing pipelines left the data almost
unchanged, and sign stability in those trials is not evidence of anything. And 395
principled specifications over 16 trials, between 3 and 140 per trial, is a coarse
sampling of the space of analyses; nine of the 16 trials contribute fewer than ten. The
claim is about the typical case in a small, self-selected corpus.

### 4.5 Reporting

The reproduction rate is bounded above by what the papers state. Nine of 16 papers name
no primary outcome, and the reader has to take the first outcome of the abstract; four
report a p-value or an F statistic without an estimate of the arm difference; one states
what quantity it is estimating. CONSORT has asked for a defined primary outcome and an
effect size with its precision since 2010 [Schulz_2010], and the 2025 revision asks for
the metric and the timepoint of every outcome [Hopewell_2025]; the estimands framework
asks for the quantity itself [ICH_2019_E9R1; Kahan_2024]. Thirteen papers test baseline
balance, a practice Senn argued against in 1994 [Senn_1994], and three let the test
decide the adjustment, which makes the analysis depend on a random event. None reports
what it did with outliers. A multiverse can vary what a paper leaves open; it cannot
recover what a paper does not report.

### 4.6 Auditor degrees of freedom

Mapping a published analysis onto a deposited dataset is itself an analysis with
choices: which column is the outcome, which value is the control arm, which timepoint,
which covariates. Our first mapping, made study by study, differed from the rule-based
mapping in 13 of 16 trials and reversed the direction of the effect in seven. Rerunning
it through the final pipeline changed neither the median size of the effects nor their
range; it changed their sign. This is the garden of forking paths on the reproducer's side
[Gelman_2014; Gelman_Loken_2013]. Brodeur and colleagues let reproducers choose their
robustness checks and counted the results; the many-analysts studies show what such
freedom does to conclusions [Silberzahn_2018; Breznau_2022]. Fixed rules, written before
the mapping and applied to every trial, and a second coder who works blind, are the
remedy, and the error rate of the first pass belongs in the report.

### 4.7 Limitations

The corpus is self-selected. Trials that share data are larger, more recent and better
conducted than trials that do not [Tsujimoto_2020]. Wicherts and colleagues found more
reporting errors and weaker evidence among authors who did not share their data
[Wicherts_2011]; the association with errors was not confirmed in a conceptual repetition
[Nuijten_2017] and failed a replication [Claesen_2023]. The reproduction rate is therefore
plausibly an upper bound and the fragility estimate a lower bound for the field; no
method extrapolates from this corpus to trials that did not share.

Sixteen trials, one of them without any equivalent choice that can move the estimate,
support statements
about a typical case and a maximum, not about a distribution. Only continuous primary
outcomes were analysed; crossover and within-person designs were excluded; three of the
16 papers are secondary reports whose headline estimate is not the trial's prespecified
primary analysis. The corpus came from one database with one search string. A second
route through Zenodo and Dryad found 127 study-like deposits that were not matched to
articles. The audit was not preregistered, the mapping rules were fixed after the
published analyses had been read, and one coder has mapped the trials to date. The
integrity screen has low power at this size. The variance partition is additive and
treats confounded node pairs as separate; the interaction sensitivity analysis did not
change the dominant node, but it was possible in ten of 32 trial-by-grid combinations
only. Baseline handling was kept in the principled grid although its options differ in
precision, and almost the whole principled range is attributable to it. Instrument-
specific minimal important differences were not collected, and the 0.2 standard
deviation threshold is a convention. Pooled estimation over specifications and
multiplicity control over specifications were not attempted [Bartos_2025_arXiv;
Mandl_2024].

### 4.8 Implications

For journals and repositories, a deposit should be accepted when it contains a labelled
allocation variable, a variable named as the primary outcome, and a dictionary; without
them the deposit meets the policy and fails its purpose. For multiverse practice,
classify every decision before computing, collapse specifications that give the same
result, fix the standardisation denominator before preprocessing, and report the
principled and the defensible grid side by side, because their ratio is the finding. For
meta-research in fields without a mandate, about one trial in nine with a repository link
(11%, Wilson interval 7 to 16%) reaches recomputation, and about one in twelve (8%, 5 to
13%) reproduces; when recomputation is possible, it works.

## 5. Conclusion

In a field without a data-sharing mandate, 16 of 152 trials with a repository link
allowed a reader to recompute the published primary analysis unaided. The loss occurred
in two steps, and the second, from readable data to identifiable analysis, is the one
that availability policies do not address. Where recomputation was possible, 10 of 12
published estimates were recovered to 2% or 0.02 standard deviations. Over choices that
leave the question unchanged, the standardised effect moved by a median of 0.15 standard
deviations, by 1.08 in one trial of 20 participants, and kept its sign in 15 of 16 trials;
the handling of the baseline value moved it most. Choices that change the estimand widened
the range by a third. Nine papers named no primary outcome and four gave no estimate.
Our own first mapping of analyses onto data reversed seven effects before rules were
fixed, which is a finding about reanalysis as much as about the trials.
